Routing Signal Strength and Token Predictability
in Mixture-of-Experts Language Models

Higher Criticism of Expert Routing P-values as a Predictor of CE Loss and Intelligence Gain
May 2026  ·  Internal Research Report
Abstract. We study whether the Higher Criticism (HC) statistic — applied to per-expert routing P-values in Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) language models — predicts per-token cross-entropy (CE) loss and, crucially, the intelligence gain: the reduction in CE loss attributable to a larger model relative to a smaller model in the same family. HC captures the presence of a sparse anomaly among experts (a few specialists firing far from their null behaviour), while the Fisher combination statistic captures dense, globally elevated signal. We evaluate two model families (Qwen MoE, Gemma 4), two benchmarks (lambada, hellaswag), six null estimators, and four HC aggregation strategies. Our central finding is a consistent negative correlation between HC and intelligence gain for Qwen on lambada: anomalous routing does not flag tokens where scale helps. Gemma exhibits a sign reversal of the HC–CE relationship, tentatively attributed to its permanently activated shared expert. HC adds predictive power beyond Fisher after partialling, and per-expert aggregation carries substantially more signal than map-level aggregation.

1. Research Goals

This study asks five concrete questions about MoE expert routing signals and their relationship to language model performance:

  1. Token difficulty encoding. Does HC of the routing P-values correlate with the per-token CE loss of an MoE model? Do tokens that activate anomalous expert subsets also tend to be harder to predict?
  2. Scale asymmetry. Does the same HC statistic predict CE loss differently for large vs. small models in the same family, sharing the same tokeniser and evaluated on identical data?
  3. Intelligence gain. Does HC predict the token-level reduction in CE loss when moving from a small model to a large one (Diff = Small CE − Big CE)? We treat Diff as a proxy for the benefit of scale ("intelligence gain").
  4. HC beyond Fisher. Does HC retain predictive power for CE loss and Diff after partialling out the Fisher combination statistic, which captures broad (non-sparse) routing deviations?
  5. Robustness. Are the correlations stable across six null estimators (GMM, GLM, StudentT, StudentTSkewed, KDE, JohnsonSU) and across per-expert vs. aggregated HC variants?

2. Nomenclature

Term / SymbolDefinition
CE loss Cross-entropy (negative log-probability) loss of a model on a single token. Lower CE = better prediction.
Big CE CE loss of the larger model in the family pair (Qwen MoE large, or Gemma 4 26B-A4B).
Small CE CE loss of the smaller model in the same family (Qwen Small MoE, or Gemma 4 5B dense).
Diff — Intelligence Gain Diff = Small CE − Big CE. Positive values indicate tokens where the large model predicts better. Treated as a proxy for the benefit of increased scale ("intelligence gain"). Both models share the same tokeniser and are evaluated on identical token sequences.
HC — Higher Criticism Higher Criticism statistic applied to the vector of per-expert routing P-values for a given token. High HC signals a sparse anomaly: a few experts activated far more (or less) than their null behaviour predicts. HC is the strength of the sparse signal; it does not require many experts to be anomalous, only a few extreme ones.
Fisher Fisher combination statistic (sum of −2 log p-values) applied to the same expert routing P-values. High Fisher signals a dense anomaly: many experts are collectively elevated beyond their null. Fisher and HC are complementary: the former captures broad deviations, the latter captures concentrated ones.
Max Per Row The maximum HC value across expert dimensions for a given token (row). Summarises the single strongest expert-level anomaly per token.
per_expert HC HC computed from P-values derived from individual expert routing probabilities — one P-value per expert. Captures which specific experts are anomalous for a token.
map_agg HC HC computed from routing statistics that have been aggregated (mapped) at a coarser level before the HC test. Less granular than per-expert; dilutes individual expert anomalies.
_standard / _hat Two variants of P-value computation: standard uses the raw routing statistic; hat uses an empirically corrected (estimated) version. Results are qualitatively similar across both variants.
Null estimator The parametric distribution fitted to expert routing signals under the null (no anomaly). Six estimators are compared: GMM, GLM, StudentT, StudentTSkewed, KDE, JohnsonSU.
Pearson_Diff Pearson correlation between HC (max per row) and Diff, computed across all tokens in the dataset.
Partial_Pearson_Diff Pearson correlation between HC and Diff after partialling out (controlling for) the Fisher statistic. Isolates the unique predictive contribution of the sparse signal (HC) beyond the dense signal (Fisher).
_Q50 / _Q90 Correlations computed on the subset of tokens with HC above the 50th or 90th percentile respectively. Probes whether the HC–CE relationship concentrates at the most anomalous tokens.

Finding 1 — Only Qwen/lambada Shows a Meaningful HC–Intelligence Gain Signal

Of the four scenarios, only Qwen on lambada produces a large, consistent Intelligence Gain correlation (Diff ≈ −0.13). Gemma on lambada shows near-zero Diff correlation despite showing HC–CE correlations of its own. Both model pairs on hellaswag show weak correlations throughout. The lambada format — next-word prediction with rich context — appears to be necessary for the signal to emerge.

Finding 2 — HC–CE Asymmetry: Small Model Is More Sensitive to Routing Anomalies

For Qwen on lambada, Pearson(HC, Small CE) ≈ 0.15–0.23 consistently exceeds Pearson(HC, Big CE) ≈ 0.06–0.13 across all six estimators. Both are positive — tokens with anomalous expert routing (high HC) are harder for both models — but the small model suffers proportionally more at those tokens. This asymmetry is robust to all null estimator choices.

Finding 3 — HC Anti-correlates with Intelligence Gain (Qwen/lambada)

The Pearson correlation between HC and Intelligence Gain (Diff = Small CE − Big CE) is consistently negative for Qwen on lambada: approximately −0.13 to −0.16 for per-expert HC variants. This is the central finding: tokens where expert routing is most anomalous (sparse signal, high HC) are precisely the tokens where the large model has less advantage over the small model.

Interpreted under the HC lens: the presence of a few exceptionally specialised experts does not predict where scaling provides the most benefit. The intelligence gain of the large model appears to concentrate on tokens that look routinely handled from a routing perspective, not on tokens that trigger unusual expert selection.

The Q50 subset (tokens with HC above the median) shows an even stronger anti-correlation (≈ −0.21 for GMM), while the Q90 subset weakens to ≈ −0.07, suggesting the signal is most prominent in the upper-middle HC range rather than the very extremes.

Finding 4 — per_expert HC Carries Far More Signal than map_agg

The per-expert HC (both _standard and _hat variants) yields strong positive correlations with CE loss and a clear negative correlation with Intelligence Gain. The map_agg HC variants show near-zero or negative correlations with CE loss, and a weaker (but still negative) correlation with Diff. This indicates that the informative signal is individual-expert-level routing anomaly: coarser aggregation dilutes it. Future work should prioritise per-expert HC.

Finding 5 — HC Has Unique Predictive Power Beyond Fisher

After removing the variance explained by the Fisher statistic, HC still correlates negatively with Intelligence Gain: partial Pearson ≈ −0.09 to −0.15 (Qwen/lambada, per_expert). Fisher and HC are complementary: the dense signal (many experts collectively elevated) and the sparse signal (a few experts strongly anomalous) each independently predict where the large model's advantage is diminished. The sparse structure of routing — not merely its overall intensity — matters.

Figure 5 shows the same analysis for Gemma 4 (26B-A4B vs. 5B) on lambada, using the GMM estimator across all four HC file types.

HC ↔ Big CE
HC ↔ Small CE
HC ↔ Intelligence Gain
Figure 5. Gemma/lambada (GMM estimator): Pearson correlations for four HC file types. In contrast to Qwen, high HC predicts lower CE loss (sign reversal) and near-zero Intelligence Gain correlation. The map_agg variants show the strongest negative CE correlations.

Finding 6 — Gemma Sign Reversal: High HC Predicts Easier Tokens

In Gemma, per-expert HC correlates slightly negatively with CE loss (Big and Small) for most estimators, and map_agg HC shows strong negative correlations (≈ −0.20 to −0.25). This is the opposite of Qwen: high HC in Gemma flags tokens that are easier, not harder. Additionally, HC carries essentially no information about where the large Gemma model outperforms the small one (Intelligence Gain correlation ≈ 0 throughout).

Both Qwen and Gemma use token-choice top-k routing, ruling out routing paradigm as the explanation. The most architecturally distinctive feature of Gemma 4 26B is its permanently activated shared expert (3× the size of regular experts, always on regardless of routing). This may absorb general predictive work; the routed experts then specialise in narrower patterns. Under this hypothesis, high HC in Gemma flags tokens that are already well-served by the shared expert — thus easy — while the routed expert anomaly reflects specialisation rather than difficulty. This hypothesis is directly testable (see Open Questions, Q2).

Finding 7 — Hellaswag Produces Weak, Inconsistent Patterns

On hellaswag, correlations between HC and all CE metrics are substantially smaller in magnitude and inconsistent in sign across estimators and HC file types. The Intelligence Gain correlation for Qwen on hellaswag is approximately +0.013 on average — near zero and of opposite sign to lambada. For Gemma on hellaswag, small positive correlations appear for Big/Small CE but again near-zero Diff.

The dataset dependence points to a structural prerequisite: lambada's next-word prediction format generates high token-level CE variance and rich contextual routing signals. Hellaswag's shorter sentence-completion format may not produce sufficient token-level CE variability for the HC–loss coupling to emerge. Notably, an anomalously large Avg_HC value (≈ 10.9) appeared for StudentT on hellaswag/Qwen in one configuration, suggesting potential numerical instability of the StudentT estimator in low-signal environments.

5. Open Questions & How to Settle Them

High Priority

Q1. Does HC provide signal beyond the single most extreme expert p-value (−log pmin)?

HC is designed to detect sparse signals distributed across several anomalous experts. However, if the routing anomaly is consistently dominated by a single expert, HC essentially reduces to −log pmin — the negative log of the smallest p-value in the expert vector. In that case, all observed HC correlations with CE loss and Intelligence Gain would be attributable to one outlier expert, not a genuine multi-expert sparse structure. Distinguishing these two explanations is critical: the former implies a single specialist drives token difficulty; the latter implies a coordinated set of specialists does.

Settlement: (a) Compute Pearson(−log pmin, CE) and Pearson(−log pmin, Diff) for all 24 configurations and compare directly with the corresponding HC correlations. If the values are nearly identical, HC is acting as a proxy for the dominant expert alone. (b) Compute the partial correlation Pearson(HC, Diff | −log pmin): if it vanishes after controlling for pmin, the multi-expert structure adds nothing. (c) Examine the empirical per-token distribution of the number of p-values falling below a significance threshold (e.g., 0.01, Bonferroni-corrected): if it is almost always 1, the data do not support a multi-expert sparse interpretation regardless of the HC magnitude. (d) Conversely, run the same correlation analysis using HC computed on the P-value vector with pmin removed — if the signal survives, the second and further experts genuinely contribute.
High Priority

Q2. Does the small model's own HC show the same anti-correlation with Intelligence Gain?

All current correlations use the big model's routing HC. If both models' HCs anti-correlate with Diff equally, the effect is a property of the token. If only the big model's HC does, it is a property of scale-specific routing.

Settlement: Compute HC from the small model's routing signals and compute Pearson(HC_small, Diff). Compare to Pearson(HC_big, Diff). A Fisher z-test on the difference of correlations establishes whether the asymmetry is significant. Run for all 24 estimator × HC-file configurations on Qwen/lambada.
High Priority

Q3. Are the Pearson correlations statistically significant?

Values of −0.13 to −0.16 look meaningful but we have not established significance against a token-shuffle null, nor accounted for autocorrelation in the CE sequence.

Settlement: (a) Permute HC values against CE loss and Diff 10 000 times; build an empirical null distribution and report achieved p-values. (b) Estimate effective sample size using the autocorrelation function of the CE loss sequence (adjacent tokens are correlated within a passage). (c) Apply a block-bootstrap respecting passage boundaries to compute confidence intervals on Pearson(HC, Diff).
Medium Priority

Q4. Does the HC–Diff signal concentrate in specific Diff quantiles?

The Q50/Q90 HC-subset analyses hint at non-linearity, but the direction is ambiguous. We need a clean conditional analysis by Diff decile.

Settlement: Bin tokens into Diff deciles. Compute Pearson(HC, Diff) within each decile and plot the profile. If the anti-correlation strengthens in the top deciles (largest intelligence gain), HC identifies a regime where scale helps but routing is anomalous — a potential contradiction. If it weakens, the anti-correlation is driven by the bulk of tokens where Diff is near zero.
Medium Priority

Q5. Does the signal replicate on a third dataset with different characteristics?

The lambada vs. hellaswag contrast might be due to format (next-word vs. completion), domain, or CE variance. A third dataset would disambiguate.

Settlement: Run the full pipeline on (a) WikiText-103 (long-document causal LM, high CE variance, similar format to lambada) and (b) a reasoning-heavy dataset such as MMLU or ARC. If WikiText-103 shows a similar pattern to lambada, format and CE variance are the key factors. If MMLU shows a similar pattern despite different format, domain knowledge is key.
